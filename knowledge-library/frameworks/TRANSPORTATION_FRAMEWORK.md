# Hustle City Transportation Framework

**Status:** Authority split and local operating environments approved. Detailed network design remains open.

## 1. Two separate railway authorities

### Hustle International Metro (HIM) — intercity
HIM carries passengers between Hustle City and other cities/regions. Destinations discussed include Chicago, Atlanta, New York and Florida destinations, with room for future playable locations.
- HIM is the long-distance passenger railway.
- HIM provides the intercity arrival experience used in the opening sequence.
- Frank Maserati and his female travel companion arrive by intercity passenger train; this belongs to HIM.
- A major Hustle City terminal provides arrivals, departures, passenger services and transfers. Whether additional intercity terminals are needed remains open.

### Hustle City Transit Authority (HCTA) — local
HCTA moves passengers within Hustle City: the six central districts, downtown, neighborhoods and surrounding/outer areas.
Its rail network supports:
1. **Elevated rail** — elevated structures and stations.
2. **Underground rail** — tunnels, subway stations and underground interchanges.
3. **Street-level rail** — at-grade corridors, dedicated rights-of-way and suitable street-running sections.

A route may transition between environments only where its selected rail technology and engineering permit. Do not assume every train type can safely use every track type.

## 2. Outer neighborhood access and transfers
- Every designated outer neighborhood box receives an HCTA station or defined passenger connection.
- HCTA connects outer neighborhoods to the central districts.
- Selected outer or central stations may be HIM/HCTA interchange locations.
- An HCTA neighborhood stop does not automatically need a full HIM intercity platform.
- Player journey: HIM arrival → central/interchange terminal → HCTA local line → outer neighborhood station → destination. The reverse journey must work.
- Reserve station footprints, access paths, track corridors, service access and pedestrian connections in the master geography before placing buildings.

## 3. Network planning requirements
Specify line identifiers, colors, names and roles; route geometry and station sequence; district/neighborhood coverage; transfers; track type and elevation changes; gradients, curves, switches and right-of-way; train type, capacity, platform length, door positions, power and signaling assumptions; service frequency, operating hours, dwell times and passenger capacity; accessibility, emergency egress, ventilation, fire/life safety and security; and connections to other mobility systems.

Chicago's color-coded lines are a familiarity reference, not a requirement to copy CTA routes or branding. Denver-style mixed urban rail environments are a reference for varied right-of-way conditions, not a literal track plan.

## 4. Station and environment quality bar
Stations and rail environments must be convincing, high-quality 3D urban architecture—not generic or toy-like props. Include credible structural systems, track geometry, platform clearances and train scale; concrete, steel, glass, masonry, asphalt, tile, tactile surfaces and realistic wear; detailed entrances, concourses, platforms, stairs, elevators, escalators and accessible routes; signage, wayfinding, route maps, digital arrival boards, ticketing, lighting, cameras and passenger amenities; distinct architecture for downtown, neighborhood, waterfront, elevated, underground and street-level locations; and day/night/weather variations.

## 5. Living stations and construction
Stations may be operating, partially operating, closed, renovated or under construction. Construction scenes can include workers, engineers, supervisors, inspectors, track crews, electricians, concrete crews, cranes, excavators, trucks, scaffolding, barriers, temporary walkways, deliveries and active work zones. Equipment, materials, work progress, safety barriers and temporary passenger routes must match the construction phase.

## 6. NPC requirements
Station populations include commuters, visitors, operators, attendants, ticket staff, security, cleaners, maintenance teams, vendors and construction personnel. Give them roles, schedules, tasks, destinations and context-aware behaviors. See the Sims/MPC/NPC framework.

## 7. Naming conventions
Combine familiar American/Chicago-style geographic naming patterns (avenues, parks, squares, markets, lakefronts, junctions, exchanges), Miami/Florida-inspired waterfront, tropical, coastal and commercial character where appropriate, and original Hustle City neighborhoods, landmarks and fictional place names. Use original fictional names and geography. Brainstormed names remain proposals until approved.

Maintain a station register with stable station ID, official name, pronunciation if needed, district, neighborhood, served lines, station type, construction state, transfer links and naming rationale.

## 8. Station specification record
Capture station ID/name; authority and line(s); district/neighborhood and map reference; elevation classification; typology and architectural identity; platforms, access and interchange paths; construction/operational phase; NPC roles and activities; accessibility, safety and service requirements; visual references and source links; approval status and revision history.

## 9. Open decisions
Line count, route alignments, line colors, final station names, terminal count, outer-box station layout, train technology, fares, timetables and construction sequencing remain open.
